My bet is that in **LSHC (Life Sciences & Healthcare), evals are the new validation**. Regulated industries have always asked software for evidence that it is fit for its intended use. With deterministic software, that evidence was a test script run once. With AI, outputs vary and models drift, so the evidence becomes a living eval suite: tied to a context of use, sized to risk, and re-run whenever the model, prompt or data changes. Regulators and industry bodies are already moving this way, and the AI products that win in LSHC will be the ones designed to show their evidence, admit uncertainty, and know when to stop.

This profile is where I work in public: real prototypes, the decisions behind them, and the frameworks I use.

### Principles

**Design the "no" first.** In LSHC, a confident wrong answer does more harm than no answer. So before I design what an AI says, I decide what it must not say, and how it can still help when it says no.

**Make the evidence visible.** Every answer should show where it came from. Trust you can't inspect isn't trust.

**Calm is a feature.** People arrive at LSHC software anxious or under pressure. Dense information should feel quiet, not loud.

**Opinionated defaults.** The best tools decide for you, so you only make the decisions that matter.

**Taste is what you leave out.** Every feature I cut makes the ones I keep easier to trust.

**If you can't test it, you can't ship it.** Requirements without acceptance criteria are wishes. That was true for validated systems, and it's true for LLMs.

**Build for the people the market skipped.** The bigger AI opportunity sits further down the pyramid, with the clinic, the caregiver and the small manufacturer.
